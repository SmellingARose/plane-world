Eddies

Swirls of air of every size. Big ones break into smaller ones until viscosity turns the tiniest into heat. Calculating them all needs cells smaller than the smallest, which is why most solvers model the small ones.

The maths

Navier–Stokes: the exact physics▸
∂u∂t+(u·∇)u=−1ρ∇p+ν∇2u,∇·u=0
What it says
For every tiny parcel of air: its acceleration (left side) equals the push from pressure differences plus the drag of neighbouring air through viscosity. The second equation says air is not created or destroyed.
Where it comes from
Newton's second law (F = ma) and conservation of mass applied to a small box of fluid, plus Stokes' 1845 rule that a fluid's internal friction is proportional to how fast it is being sheared.
How it is solved
No general pen-and-paper solution exists (proving one exists is a Millennium Prize problem). Every method below solves it, or a simplified version of it, numerically on a grid of cells.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
Air is a continuum (true above about a micrometre). Friction is proportional to shear rate (true for air and water). This incompressible form also assumes constant density, valid below about Mach 0.3; the full compressible version adds density and an energy equation.
Versus the exact physics
This is the reference. Everything below is measured against it.
Splitting the flow: calculated + modelled▸
u=ucalc⏟on the grid+umodel⏟smaller than a cellτij=−ρ⟨ui′uj′⟩≈2ρνmodelSij
What it says
Split the velocity into the part a solver calculates on its grid and the part it cannot see. The unseen part still pushes the seen part around; that push is τ (the turbulent stress), and it is approximated as extra viscosity times the strain rate S.
Where it comes from
Osborne Reynolds (1895) split flow into mean and fluctuation. Putting u = ū + u′ into the (u·∇)u term and averaging leaves a new unknown ⟨u′u′⟩: more unknowns than equations, called the closure problem. Boussinesq (1877) proposed treating it like viscosity.
How it is solved
Each solver supplies its own formula for νmodel; that formula is the whole difference between them.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
Boussinesq: turbulence mixes momentum the same in every direction and lines up with the mean shear. Reasonable in thin attached boundary layers; poor in swirling, strongly curved or separated flow.
Versus the exact physics
Exact if nothing is modelled (DNS). Every model adds error proportional to how much of the turbulence it hands to the formula.

S is the strain rate, how fast the flow is being stretched or sheared: Sij = ½(∂ui/∂xj + ∂uj/∂xi). Overbar = averaged, tilde = filtered (smoothed over one cell).

Potential flow▸
∇2ϕ=0
What it says
Air velocity is the slope of a single smooth field φ, and that field satisfies Laplace's equation. Pressure then follows from Bernoulli: faster air means lower pressure, which is where lift comes from.
Where it comes from
Navier–Stokes with viscosity set to zero (inviscid) and no spinning of fluid parcels anywhere (irrotational). Both drop out the hard terms and leave a linear equation.
How it is solved
Cover the surface with panels carrying sources and vortices, solve one linear system (seconds), and add the Kutta condition: flow must leave the trailing edge smoothly, which fixes how much circulation, and therefore lift, the wing has.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
No viscosity, no eddies, no boundary layer. Vorticity exists only in thin wake sheets.
Versus the exact physics
No friction drag at all and no stall: an infinite wing would feel zero drag (d'Alembert's paradox). Lift and induced drag are good while flow stays attached.
Steady RANS▸
(u‾·∇)u‾=−∇p‾/ρ+∇·[(ν+νt)2S‾]
What it says
The time-averaged flow is in balance: the averaged convection of momentum equals pressure plus viscous plus turbulent (νt) stress. Nothing changes in time.
Where it comes from
Average Navier–Stokes over time (Reynolds), close the extra term with Boussinesq, then compute νt from one or two more transport equations. Spalart–Allmaras (1992) carries one quantity; k-ω SST (Menter 1994) carries turbulent kinetic energy k and its dissipation rate ω, using k-ω near walls and k-ε away from them, with a limiter (the max(...) in its νt) that stops it over-predicting shear stress before separation.
How it is solved
Finite volumes on a mesh; march in pseudo-time with an implicit solver and multigrid until the residuals stop changing. That converged field is the answer.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
The flow has a steady average. Boussinesq holds. Model constants were tuned on flat plates, pipes and mixing layers, so they fit those best.
Versus the exact physics
Throws away every fluctuation and replaces it with a calibrated guess. Attached flow: a few percent. Where flow separates, the separation point depends on the calibration, so stall angle and maximum lift are often wrong.
URANS▸
∂u‾∂t+RANS
What it says
Same as RANS but the averaged flow may change slowly in time, so large regular unsteadiness (vortex shedding, wake flapping) can appear.
Where it comes from
Average over many repeats of the same experiment (ensemble) instead of over time, keeping ∂ū/∂t.
How it is solved
Dual time stepping: each real time step is converged like a small RANS problem; run until the forces settle into a pattern, then average them.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
A spectral gap: the unsteadiness you keep is much slower than the turbulence you model. Often untrue in separated flow.
Versus the exact physics
Captures shedding RANS misses, but the turbulence is still a formula, and its high model viscosity damps resolved eddies.
DDES▸
l=lRANS−fdmax(0,lRANS−CΔ)
What it says
One model with a switch. It uses a length scale l: the RANS value inside attached boundary layers, but the grid size CΔ where the grid can see eddies. With CΔ the model becomes a standard LES sub-grid model.
Where it comes from
Spalart's Detached-Eddy Simulation (1997); the 'delayed' version (2006) adds the shield fd ≈ 0 inside boundary layers so a fine grid cannot accidentally switch to LES there and cause fake separation.
How it is solved
Time-accurate, with low-dissipation numerical schemes in the LES regions, small time steps (CFL about 1) and long runs to average.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
RANS is good enough for the attached boundary layer. The separated region's grid is fine enough to resolve its large eddies. In the 'grey area' where the model hands over, resolved eddies take time to appear.
Versus the exact physics
Attached parts carry RANS error; separated parts are close to LES. That is why it gets stall much better (about 5 % on maximum lift in one 2024 study) at about 10× the cost.
Wall-modelled LES▸
νsgs=(CsΔ)2|S~|, wall: log law
What it says
Smooth the flow over one cell width Δ (filtering) and solve for that smoothed flow exactly. Only eddies smaller than a cell are modelled, with viscosity (CsΔ)²|S̃|. At the wall, the log law supplies the skin friction instead of resolving the thin layer touching the surface.
Where it comes from
Smagorinsky (1963, originally for weather models) plus Kolmogorov's 1941 theory: the smallest eddies look the same in any flow, so one generic model fits them. The log law (κ ≈ 0.41) comes from Prandtl and von Kármán's analysis of boundary layers.
How it is solved
Explicit or implicit time stepping with central (low-dissipation) schemes, small steps, many passes of the flow over the body, then averaging.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
Sub-cell eddies are universal and isotropic. The boundary layer next to the wall is in equilibrium so the log law holds, which weakens near separation and strong pressure gradients.
Versus the exact physics
Only the smallest eddies and the near-wall layer are modelled, so it is close to the truth almost everywhere. Remaining error sits at the wall, where stall starts.
Wall-resolved LES▸
νsgs, Δwall:y+≈1
What it says
The same filtered equations, but the grid is fine enough at the wall to resolve the viscous layer itself, so no wall model is needed.
Where it comes from
LES theory as above, with near-wall spacing in viscous units: first cell at y⁺ ≈ 1, about 50 along the flow and 15 across it.
How it is solved
As wall-modelled LES, with far more cells and smaller time steps.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
Only Kolmogorov's universality of the smallest eddies.
Versus the exact physics
Very close to the truth; cost grows about as Re2.7, out of reach for a full aircraft.
DNS▸
∂u∂t+(u·∇)u=−∇p/ρ+ν∇2u
What it says
The Navier–Stokes equations exactly as written. Nothing is modelled.
Where it comes from
Navier–Stokes itself.
How it is solved
High-order schemes on grids reaching the Kolmogorov scale η = (ν³/ε)1/4, about 0.02 mm on the Trainer's wing, with tiny time steps.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
Only continuum and Newtonian friction, plus numerical error.
Versus the exact physics
This is the truth, at a cost growing about as Re2.9: roughly 10¹³ cells for the Trainer. Used only for physics research on small cases.
Lattice Boltzmann (LES)▸
fi(x+ciΔt,t+Δt)=fi−fi−fieqτ
What it says
Air is represented by populations fi of particles moving along fixed lattice directions. Each step they stream to the neighbour cell, then collide, relaxing toward equilibrium at rate 1/τ.
Where it comes from
The Boltzmann equation of kinetic gas theory, discretised in velocity. A Chapman–Enskog expansion shows it reproduces Navier–Stokes with viscosity ν = cs²(τ − ½)Δt.
How it is solved
Stream (shift arrays) and collide (local maths): no pressure equation to solve, so it runs very fast on GPUs. Used as LES with a sub-grid model and wall model at practical resolutions.
Assumptions (what gets dropped)
Low Mach number (errors grow as Mach²). A single relaxation time, or a multi-relaxation variant for stability. Turbulence handled by whatever LES model is attached.
Versus the exact physics
Same physics content as the LES it implements, plus compressibility error at higher speeds. Our removed real-time solver was this, on grids far too coarse.
